<h2> Lessons about Impostor Syndrome from a Pulitzer Novel</h2>

I often scour through the list of Pulitzer winners when I am looking for a new, refreshing read. One such scavenger hunt brought me to <a href="https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/31933085-less"><i>Less</i> by Andrew Sean Greer </a>, a coming of age novel but for a middle-aged gay American novelist. 

The protagonist, Arthur Less, is turning 50 and goes on a world tour to discover what it means to be a 50 year old man. He hops from flight to boat to train to bus to meet friends, former lovers, fellow writers, and insightful strangers who pepper his journey with heart warming experiences. Through Arthur's adventures across the globe we see glimpses of his past, mainly his long term relationship with a famous, "genius" but jaded older poet, which has moulded how Arthur sees himself as a writer, as a partner. Because he has been with a genius for a considerable amount of time, he has come to believe that he is no good as a writer.

Through his association with his partner, Arthur has been with so-called legends of the writing sphere, with the generational talents, with prize winners and trend setters. In such haloed company of giants, nay stars of their fields, then, how can anyone see their own shine. At an early point in the book a character asks Arthur how it feels to know he will only ever be mediocre.

Even if you don't yourself compare with your cohort or group-mates, a comparison is often forced through the system and is often inescapable. You are always surrounded by excellent, talented people who inspire you to become a better scientists and who apply for the same positions as you. Jobs are few, permanent positions fewer still, and the insane levels of competition means being good, like Arthur was, isn't enough. You have to be better than good, perhaps even great?

As we trundle through academia, we tend to forget. Like Arthur we develop amnesia about our own wins, our hard work and perseverance, our intelligence and creativity, all of which brought us where are now from where we were when we started. Like Arthur, we forget about our own shine in the brilliance of geniuses and greatness. 

When you read Less, to you, the reader, it is abundantly clear that Arthur is charming, affable and with decent writing skills which receive acknowledgement from his peers. Yet, Arthur himself is oblivious to his own virtues. So you might get irritated, like I did, "This person is great! How can they not see how amazing their own qualities are?"

And then a light bulb moment. Like myself you will have arrived at the definition of impostor syndrome.
