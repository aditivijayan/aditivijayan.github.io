title: Human-side of academia

<p>When I started my PhD in 2015, I had, wrongly assumed that research is more science than people. In my mind was the very stereotypical image of a scientist, perhaps Sherlock Holmes-esque, analytical, rational, wholly and solely concerned with science. But over the past decade I've realised that science is more people than an outsider would have perceived. So, here's my list of human aspects of science that took me a long while to realise.
</p>

<h3>1. No people, no science.</h3>

Gone are the days of a lone scientist discovering the laws of nature in solitude. Increasingly science in general, astronomy in particular, is becoming collaborative in nature. Without supportive professional networks having a successful career is exceedingly difficult. You have to cultivate relationships!

The most important relationship you can cultivate in academia is between you and your PhD advisor. It will, to a great extent, determine the trajectory of your career and entirely determine how satisfying your PhD journey is. What makes an advisor-student relationship successful depends a lot on the individuals and little on their science goals. 

How expectations are communicated, how conflicts are managed, what the student needs and what the supervisor is able to provide are people-problems no code can solve. While both you and your supervisor might be passionate about science, non-science aspects like long-term goals, working styles, ambitions, and even personal commitments sometimes take centre-stage. 

Investing in relationships is how we build networks.

<h3>2. Make friends, lose friends.</h3>

Joining Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore, meant I was surrounded by people who were the best, the creme de la creme of India. These people were passionate about their fields and had a broad set of interests. I met people who had read the same books as myself, who were interested in philosophy, music, writing. People I met during my PhD have inspired me, nurtured me, encouraged me, and supported me. Their achievements in turn were the fuel for my aspirations. Covid was liveable because of the friends I found.

And over the years, I have lost them. Academia brought us together and academia builds the distance between us. New jobs, new cities, new time zone. Yes, there is whatsapp and zoom. But life has a way of getting in the way. 

Sometimes you'll be lucky and you'll find new friends, new groups. Other times the city will suck, the weather will suck. You will be nostalgic and harbour disdain for a bleak town in the middle of nowhere or nurse a grudge towards very busy, apathetic city with none of the warmth you miss so dearly.


<h3>3. Academia changes people, changes destinations.</h3>

When I started my PhD the whole cohort wanted a career in academia. We all wanted a job in a university, preferably the ones we graduated from. We wanted to teach physics, mentor students. Give back to the system that brought us where we are. Follow our passion for astronomy! 

Now? Now, we have seen how academia works. We have seen jobs are few and so hard to come by. We have seen our school mates in non-academic professions buy homes, "settle down", find a forever place, and adult. 

Academia becomes exhausting, to look for a new job every few years, to pack your bags and build your life elsewhere. Home beckons and we yearn for stability, predictability. 

What then, you may ask, was the point of all this- five years of PhD and then years of postdoc, of inter-continental moves, of leaving your loved ones behind? 

Well, dear reader, the destination was never the point. The journey was.

<h3>4. You might end up at the same place but the journeys are different.</h3>

Now that I'm a postdoc, some might even say mid-career, I look around to other postdocs. All of my fellow postdocs have had such different journeys, there isn't a common ground. 

Some of us, like myself, took a break at some point in their careers to pursue a traditional job and returned to academia. Some didn't and had a straightforward trajectory. 

Some have stuck to their fields and groups for years while some have bounced from field to field, from group to group. Some of us have moved twice, thrice. Some graduated from one place and just never left. 

Some found mentors and networks that helped them flourish. Some forged their own paths.

Who's to say what works. Who's to say what success looks like. 

This is why I don't like one-size-fits-all advice about academia- <i>you're not publishing/conferencing/mentoring/seminar-ing/collaborating enough</i>. It is really difficult to say what enough is. 

Sometimes enough is just being at the right place at the right time, knowing the right people. Sometimes even being the best isn't enough.

Academia is as much a human experience as it is a science endeavour. Our expectations, ambitions, environment, interactions all determine how fulfilling our careers are. While there are courses, advice, online lectures, books for the former, there aren't any for the latter. 

We all figure it out in our own ways.
